Hazards

The Chomper follows a four-beat attack, so it reads before it hits.

Boss: Magmaw. In Skyline City it swipes fire waves, low or high. Jump or stay down. Every dodge fires a counter-attack at its health bar.

Boss: Tiki Titan. On Tiki Isle it punches flaming boulders. A rolling one means jump, a flying one means duck. Knock its health to zero to win.

Plus crates, hanging barriers, spikes, waterfall gaps and rooftop drops. You have 3 hearts, so one mistake doesn't end the run.

Play anywhere

Two ways to hold it

Hold the phone upright and the bottom half turns into a handheld console. Turn it sideways for the full view with touch buttons along the bottom edge. Turning the phone mid-run pauses the game.

  • Keyboard

    Space jump, Z special, ←→ move, ↓ duck or ground pound, P pause.

  • Game controllers

    Switch Pro, Xbox or PlayStation: A/B jump, X/Y special, D-pad or stick to move, Start pause.

How I made it

From character sheets to a playable game

I approached this the way I'd lead a character team: I set the creative direction, made the art, reviewed every build and decided what to change. I used Claude as my engineer, writing the code and processing my art into game-ready assets.

What I did

Concept and creative direction, character design, the five-character lineup, run-cycle and action-pose frames, the environment, enemy, power-up and vehicle concepts, game design and playtesting notes.

What Claude did

Game code and physics, cutting and cleaning my frames (background removal, halo cleanup, frame alignment), splitting the environment into layers, synthesized sound, and tuning from my feedback.

  1. Start from the lineup

    The project began with a modular avatar sheet in the style of Roblox and Fortnite creator games: the five runners, the Tiki Titan and two characters still in development, plus swappable heads and expressions, outfits, accessories and silhouette tests to keep every shape readable.

    The first prototype animated this sheet with a cut-out puppet rig. It moved, but a front-facing character never reads as running in a side-scroller, so I went back and created real side-view animation.

  2. Render side-view animation

    For each character I created 14 side-view frames on one sheet: an 8-frame run cycle on top, and six action poses below (take-off crouch, rise, tuck, fall, landing and hit).

    The frames came back with background-removal fringe and gray halos. Each frame was cleaned, then aligned on the torso so the loop doesn't slide side to side.

    What I learned: a consistent sheet layout made every character after Ace go in quickly.

  3. Turn a painting into a level

    I created each world as one panorama. It was split into a softened background that scrolls slowly for depth, a tileable ground ledge that moves with the action, and the waterfall gap, which became a hazard.

    I designed the second world, Skyline City, on the same layout as the castle, so it dropped into the same pipeline. You pick which world to run on the home screen, and the All 3 option crossfades through every world as you go.

    Early playtests showed the painted platforms looked jumpable, so the background got blur and haze, and the real platforms got a bright rim.

  4. Design on paper, then build each system

    Each concept sheet became a working system: the gameplay sheet set the HUD, combos and routes, the Chomper sheet defined its idle, detect, lunge and bite beats, and the abilities and vehicle sheets became power-ups and rides.

    I also cut what didn't earn its place. A Double Jump power-up became +1 Jump because everyone already double jumps, and Star Multiplier was folded into the combo system.

  5. Design an original boss

    Skyline City needed a threat bigger than the Chomper. I designed Magmaw, a lava-rock drake, as an 8-frame sheet: stance, a three-frame wind-up, a claw swipe and a recovery.

    The swipe launches a fire wave either along the ground or at head height. The wind-up shows which one with a glowing warning band and "JUMP!" or "STAY LOW!", so every hit is fair. It attacks three times, then leaves, and you score +600 if it never touched you.

    I first blocked the fight out with placeholder art, then replaced it with my own creature design so the whole project is original work.

  6. A third world and a second boss

    I painted Tiki Isle, a tropical level with stone ruins, waterfalls and a smoking volcano, and designed the Tiki Titan, a mossy stone golem, as a 12-frame sheet: idle, a four-frame wind-up, a flaming punch and a recovery.

    Magmaw sweeps a wave across the screen; the Titan punches a single flaming boulder, rolling along the ground or flying at head height. Same fair warning, different rhythm. The boss rush now alternates the two, each in their own world.

    Playtesting asked for more control, so the home screen now lets you pick the world you run in and the boss you fight. A normal run stays in one world with no bosses; bosses only come when you choose to fight one. A route map at the top shows how far you are from the next 1,000 m mark, or the next world in All 3 mode. Bosses got a health bar: every attack you dodge launches a counter-attack star, and knocking it to zero ends the fight with a big bonus.

    Because every boss shares one state machine (enter, roar, wind-up, attack, recover, defeated), adding the Titan was mostly art: a frame list per state and a new projectile.

  7. Playtest and tune

    Playing it changed the design. Runs ended too fast, so I added 3 hearts with a stumble-and-recover instead of instant game over. New content now unlocks with distance so the first minute stays readable.

    Then came the layer that keeps people playing: a shared leaderboard, missions, a star bank with upgrades and trails, and a soundtrack generated live in the browser.

  8. Polish the first impression

    A player decides in seconds whether to keep playing, so I rebuilt the front door. The title screen became a character showcase, and Play now launches a short sequence: the runner sprints in, the zone banner drops, and a 3, 2, 1, GO! countdown starts the run.

    Then I tested it the way most people will play, on a phone. Holding it upright left the game small with empty space below, so that space became a handheld-style controller. Testing also showed the HUD covering the runner in portrait, so it now compacts on small screens.
